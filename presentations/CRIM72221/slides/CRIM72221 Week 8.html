Ethnography

What is ethnography?

 

Ethnography

systematic study of people and cultures through immersion, observation, and participation

 

\(\leadsto\) Aim: to interpret meaning, not count frequency

Key characteristics

  • Naturalistic setting (“the field”)
  • Participant observation
  • Long-term engagement
  • Reflexive relationship between researcher and subject

What is ethnography?

 

The researcher…

  • …observes and/or interacts with a study’s participants in their real-life environment
  • …represents, graphically and in writing, the culture of a group
  • …uses interviews, participant observation, and surveys

What is ethnography?

Immersive and field-based approach

  • The researcher lives among the people they study, immersing themselves within the group
  • Participates as much as possible while observing, taking detailed notes, developing an ongoing analysis from the notes, and compiling a report about the findings
  • The relationships between researcher and researched are an essential element of an ethnographic study
  • There is active sharing of knowledge between participants and researcher

Digital/virtual ethnography

Online communities

  • May be formed by people with similar interests/backgrounds
  • Can develop a culture and become sources of identification and attachment

Observing online criminal forums, hate groups, subcultures (e.g., incels)

  • Researchers…
    • …immerse themselves in the online group for an extended period
    • …keep both observational and reflective field notes and can return to the original data long after it was produced (\(\neq\) traditional ethnography)
  • The selected community should be…
    • …relevant to the research question
    • …involve frequent communication
    • …generate rich textual data

Applications in criminology

Core applications

  • Street gangs, drug markets, prisons, police cultures, online deviance
  • Accessing hidden, stigmatised, or criminalised groups

Examples

  • Goffman (2014): researcher as “roommate” in Philadelphia
  • Pearson (2009): researcher as “hooligan” in England
  • Zaitch (2002): researcher as “narco” in Colombia
  • Biondi (2016): researcher as “prison gang” in Brazil

Observation & ethnography

Observation = systematic watching and recording of social behaviour

  • Types
    • Covert
    • Overt
    • Participant
    • Non-participant

Observations

Description

  • Witnessing events first-hand to capture what actually happens
  • Spectrum: complete participant to complete observer (covert/overt)

Limitations

  • Presence can influence situations and interactions \(\leadsto\) need to triangulate
  • Can be focused or open-ended
  • Importance of field notes
Observation type Advantage Disadvantage
Participant (fieldwork) Observer sees what is happening because they take part in the process Can be dangerous for the observer
Non-participant Observer remains safe Observer cannot get a feel for what is happening
Overt Ethical Respondents may act differently
Covert Respondents act as they normally would Unethical, but…

Ethnography: Steps

 

1) Selection

The ethnographic method begins with the selection of a culture. The researcher selects the culture, community, or population according to their interest

2) Review of literature

The researcher then reviews the literature pertaining to the culture to get a brief idea and historical sketch of the culture selected for study

3) Identification of variables

The researcher then identifies variables that interest them, as well as the members of the culture, and need to be explored

Ethnography: Steps

 

4) Entry

  • The ethnographer tries to enter the culture and gain the acceptance of its members (critical)
  • Background work before entering

5) Cultural immersion

  • Ethnographers live in the culture they have chosen to study for months, or even years. The middle stages involve gaining informants, and using them to gain yet more informants in a chaining process
  • Carefully manage relationships

Ethnography: Steps

 

6) Data collection: after gaining the confidence of respondents, the researcher collects information in the form of observational transcripts and interview recordings

  • Sampling people: various subgroups are identified
  • Taking notes
    • Brief notes (jottings) \(\rightarrow\) comprehensive field notes within 24h
    • As complete, detailed, and true as possible
    • Distinguish quotes (indicate pauses, interruptions) from observations
  • Systematic observation using a checklist
  • Intensive interviews and focus groups (week 9)

Ethnography: Steps

7) Analysis of data

  • From the many sources collected, the ethnographer analyses the data for a description of the culture-sharing group, themes that emerge from the group, and an overall interpretation
  • The researcher compiles a detailed description of the culture-sharing group, focusing on a single event, on several activities, or on the group over a prolonged period of time

8) Generalisations

  • Forge a working set of rules or generalisations as to how the culture-sharing group works, as the final product of this analysis
  • The final product is a holistic cultural portrait of the group that incorporates the views of the participants (emic) as well as the views of the researcher (etic)

Skills required

The researcher needs to…

  • …have a high degree of interpretative agility to understand the wide range of issues and facts involved in the study
  • …be unbiased and unprejudiced to draw valid and reliable conclusions
  • …be familiar with the social settings, with a high degree of intellectuality and diplomacy
  • …possess good interactional and interpersonal skills
  • …be sensitive towards the culture, values, and norms of the social setting in which the study is conducted
  • …be familiar with the tenets of research ethics

Fieldwork: Do’s and don’ts

Do…

  • Have an open mind, free of any kind of prejudice or bias
  • Keep a simple stature so that people feel comfortable and act naturally
  • Be diplomatic and supportive towards the views and opinions of the subjects
  • A prior study and understanding of the group and the environment is vital
  • A good understanding of social science theory, research methods, and research design is important
  • Think about what you are going to ask beforehand and get familiar with the questions
  • During an interview, put your respondents at ease and choose a setting where they will feel safe and comfortable
  • Conduct the study in a relaxed and stress-free mood
  • Always respect the ethics and norms of the group under study

Don’t…

  • Do not interrupt; simply observe
  • Never put pressure on the study subjects
  • Do not offend the culture, values, and norms of the group under study
  • Do not overdress; keep your actions limited
  • Never distort the facts to meet the research aim
  • Never go unprepared and without prior understanding of the group
  • Never cross ethical boundaries to conduct the research

Ethnography: Strengths & weaknesses

Strengths

  • First-hand insights: as the researcher has interacted with, experienced as a participant, and observed the participants first-hand, they can be confident that the insights that surface are reliable
  • Detailed data: the length of time spent with participants, probing, and observation lead to rich and detailed data
  • High reliability: because the researcher is directly engaged with and immersed in the group, the data do not come from individuals outside the group under consideration
  • Close interaction: as a participant within the group, involved closely with other participants, the researcher can develop perceptions that give a peek into participants’ lives and experiences within their culture, and a deep understanding of different perspectives

Weaknesses

  • Time-consuming: requires a huge time commitment; for studies that go on for years, the researcher needs to stay part of the group for a long time
  • Diversity of results: the large number of participants and extended exposure to their thoughts, preferences, cultural backgrounds, and behaviour can generate large amounts of diverse data that are challenging to collate
  • Reliability: as the researcher is solely involved in the group, it is difficult to validate the reliability of the data collected and the insights generated
  • Researcher bias: as the researcher is engaged first-hand with the participants being studied, they are likely to introduce bias
  • Participant reservations: if the researcher’s role is known to the group, participants may not always be open to sharing their perspectives

Reflexivity and positionality

The researcher is part of the field—not neutral

Reflexivity means…

  • …awareness of one’s own biases, identity, and influence
  • …understanding how your presence shapes behaviour
  • …reflecting on emotional and ethical challenges (Cowburn, Gelsthorpe, and Wahidin 2017)

Reflexivity and positionality

Gender

  • A male researcher studying female sex workers might not gain the same level of trust or intimate insight as a female researcher, who may be perceived as safer or more relatable
  • Conversely, a female researcher observing male gang members might face safety issues or gendered power dynamics that limit openness

Race and ethnicity

  • A white researcher entering a racially minoritised community may be seen as an outsider or authority figure, influencing what participants are willing to share
  • A researcher from the same ethnic background may gain insider trust—but risks “going native” and losing analytic distance

Class

  • Researchers from middle-class backgrounds studying working-class or marginalised communities might misinterpret survival strategies as deviance due to differing cultural capital or assumptions

Reflexivity and positionality: A criminology example

Participant observation of football hooligan subcultures in the UK (Pearson 2009). To gain access, he had to blend in = dressing, talking, and behaving like group members

\(\leadsto\) What this shows:

  • His male identity gave him access that would likely be closed to female researchers, since violence and masculinity were central to that culture
  • His middle-class academic background sometimes made him an outsider, shaping how group members perceived him
  • Pearson’s self-awareness (reflexivity) was essential: he realised that his class and educational status made him both observer and intruder, influencing what people revealed

Reflexivity and positionality

  • Positionality shapes epistemology
    • Who we are affects what we can know and how we know it
  • Ethnography requires reflexivity
    • Being aware of how your identity, privilege, and social position shape your field relationships and the knowledge you produce
  • Reflexivity isn’t just about honesty—it’s a methodological necessity
    • We can’t remove ourselves from the field, but we can be transparent about how our identities shape what we see and what others choose to show us

Research ethics

Research ethics in criminology

  • Main issue: focus on crime
  • Offenders and victims: both a legitimate focus of research
  • Criminal justice personnel: police, judges, court officials, prison officers

\(\leadsto\) Raise complex ethical considerations

What are research ethics?

  • Moral principles that govern research and guide how we determine right and wrong
  • “Rules of fair play” in criminological research
  • Principles set out in codes of practice and research statements by organisations, e.g., funding bodies, professional associations, universities
  • Empirical research, i.e., experiments or observations to gather data from participants
  • Apply to every stage of the research process

AI ethics

Companies, governments, associations, and communities have drafted codes of ethics for AI

\(\leadsto\) Five key principles:

  • Beneficence: AI should be developed and applied to improve the well-being of our planet and its people
  • Nonmaleficence: because AI could end human life as we know it, a “do no harm” principle is critical
  • Autonomy: our ability to act freely and independently must be preserved and promoted, while the autonomy of machines must be restricted
  • Justice: AI must be developed, designed, and deployed in ways that promote justice, fairness, equity, and related values
  • Explicability: to promote the other principles, we need to know the “how” and “why” of AI systems and products. Accountability and intelligibility are key: knowing the “how” and “why” allows us to hold the correct groups responsible for the beneficial and negative impacts of AI

Why are research ethics important?

  • Ensures that research is carried out with integrity and that results have validity
  • Collective values minimise the risk of variable standards arising from the application of individual approaches to ethical issues
  • Provides guidance for researchers faced with new situations/moral dilemmas

Scenario: Moral/ethical dilemma

A criminology researcher wants to study how young people involved in street gangs use Instagram and TikTok to express identity, status, and rivalry (“digital street culture”)

During observation, the researcher finds videos showing individuals brandishing weapons and making threats—potentially evidence of criminal activity

\(\leadsto\) Should the researcher report these posts to the police, or maintain confidentiality as required by research ethics?

Ethical theories

Deontology: uses rules to distinguish right from wrong; people must follow the rules and do their duty

  • Focuses on duties and moral rules
  • e.g., Don’t lie. Don’t steal. Don’t cheat.

Consequentialism: consequences are most important (an act with good consequences is morally right)

  • Focuses on outcomes: a cost–benefit balance \(\leadsto\) which action produces the greatest overall good or least harm?
  • e.g., most people would agree that lying is wrong. But if telling a lie would help save a person’s life, consequentialism says it’s the right thing to do

Virtue ethics: based on the virtuous person (moral character) rather than the action. Live a life of moral character

  • Focuses on moral character and integrity
  • e.g., by practising being honest, brave, just, generous, and so on, a person develops an honourable and moral character

Ethical theories: Scenario

Deontology

  • The researcher’s duty is to keep confidentiality (unless immediate risk) \(\rightarrow\) don’t report

Consequentialism

  • It depends: if the post shows an imminent threat \(\rightarrow\) reporting may be the greater good
  • If not (it is symbolic) \(\rightarrow\) maintaining confidentiality and contributing to the long-term understanding of the problem is of great value to society

Virtue ethics

  • A virtuous researcher seeks balance—consulting their supervisor and the ethics board, ensuring the response is measured, transparent, and respectful

Key principles: Protection from harm

  • No exposure to unnecessary and atypical physical or psychological harm (e.g., stress, embarrassment…)
  • Extra care with vulnerable populations (e.g., victims, children, offenders)
  • Not just about “intent”

Key principles: Protection from harm

  • Conduct a rigorous risk assessment
    • Identify the potential harms posed by the research
    • Categorise the magnitude of the potential harm into one of seven harm levels on a scale (see next slide)
    • Estimate the likelihood of each potential harm

Key principles: Consent

Voluntary consent

Fundamental aspect of ethical research practice: consent obtained from participants (not influenced by pressure from staff, family)

Informed consent

  • Give information about the nature and purpose of the research
  • Risks/benefits
  • What is expected \(\leadsto\) reasoned decision about whether to take part
  • How much is “too much” or “not enough”?
  • Focus on what they need to know about involvement to make an informed decision

Key principles: Anonymity & confidentiality

Anonymity

Protection of the identity of participants: name and other personal information unknown (identities are known to the researcher only)

Confidentiality

Extent to which identifiable material is shared with others or published

Participant information sheet

  • Title of research
  • About the research
  • What would my involvement be?
  • Data protection and confidentiality
  • What if I have a complaint?
  • Research contact details

\(\leadsto\) A template: https://documents.manchester.ac.uk/DocuInfo.aspx?DocID=37214

Consent form

Statements

  • Participant information sheet
  • Voluntary and right to withdraw
  • Interviews are recorded
  • Data collected is anonymous
  • Researchers obliged to break confidentiality
  • I agree to participate

Consent form… however

  • In many projects, obtaining a signed informed consent form could jeopardise the integrity of the research and undermine the participant–researcher relationship
  • Inappropriate if the participant…
    • …wants to keep their anonymity
    • …is illiterate
    • …is sceptical of legal documents
    • …is fearful of the future implications of their participation
  • Shapes the sample and results
    • Researchers use deception to “catch” people in behaviours they would otherwise not admit to if they knew they were part of a research study (e.g., violence in prison)

Golden rule

  • Do unto others as you would have them do unto you
  • Place yourself in the “shoes” of your participants
    • How comfortable would you be with what you are asking of them?

Activity: Online/digital ethnography

Activity: Online/digital ethnography

You are a researcher studying youth violence and social media. You discover a private Discord server called HoleNet, where young people share…

  • …music videos and lyrics
  • …memes and inside jokes
  • …“digital beefs” involving coded threats
  • …photos of expensive items and, sometimes, weapons
  • Access requires an invite from a member
  • Some channels are public; others are locked

\(\leadsto\) You want to understand how status, conflict, and identity are constructed online

Activity: Online/digital ethnography

How will you enter this online field? Choose one:

  • Overt entry: you message the group admins and declare your identity as a researcher
  • Covert entry: you create an anonymous profile and attempt to join without disclosing your identity
  • Hybrid entry: you observe public channels openly but do not enter private ones

Challenge 1: The post

A user uploads a photo of themselves with a knife and writes:

“If they come to my block tomorrow, it’s over.”

\(\leadsto\) What do you do?

Challenge 1: The post

  • Assess seriousness & imminence
  • Consult supervisor/ethics board if time allows
  • Avoid reporting unless immediate danger (deontology)
  • Evaluate harm/benefit (consequentialism)

Challenge 2: The researcher identity

Some users think you’re a journalist.
Others think you’re police.
A few keep asking for your Instagram.

\(\leadsto\) What do you do?

Challenge 2: The researcher identity

  • Clarify your researcher role without compromising safety
  • Explain your purpose transparently
  • Do not provide personal accounts

Challenge 3: Community expectations

A member says:

“If you’re studying us, then promote my music in your research.”

\(\leadsto\) Do you engage? Decline? Compromise?

Challenge 3: Community expectations

  • Maintain boundaries
  • Decline to promote content; offer non-promissory alternatives (e.g., anonymised analysis)

Challenge 4: Data ownership

A member messages you:

“Delete everything you saved from me. I don’t consent anymore.”

But you already anonymised and stored screenshots months ago…

\(\leadsto\) Do you comply fully? Partially? Ignore the request because the data was public?

Challenge 4: Data ownership

  • If data is public, the legal right may remain…
  • …but ethically, honour the participant’s request when feasible
  • Remove identifiable data where possible

Challenge 5: The “lurker problem”

After two weeks in the server, someone posts:

“We’ve got too many lurkers. If you don’t post something by tonight, you’re getting kicked.”

As a researcher observing without participating…

  • …do you start posting?
  • …do you reveal your identity?
  • …do you risk being removed?

Challenge 5: The “lurker problem”

  • Withdraw from private channels
  • Observe public content only
  • Never fabricate content or deceive users

Challenge 6: The “fake criminal” problem

A user claims they “stabbed someone last summer.”
Another user says they’re lying.
A third laughs and says no one knows what’s real online.

\(\leadsto\) How do you treat these claims?

Challenge 6: The “fake criminal” problem

  • Treat content as performative, unless corroborated
  • Do not assume authenticity
  • Analyse symbolic meaning, not literal truth

Challenge 7: Data storage

You want to save screenshots for analysis.
But some include weapons, threats, and usernames.

\(\leadsto\) How do you ethically store and anonymise digital data?

Challenge 7: Data storage

  • Remove usernames
  • Store encrypted on university servers
  • Do not keep sensitive metadata

Take-away message

  • Ethnography…
    • Offline vs. online/digital
    • Requires immersion, patience, and reflexivity
    • Provides rich insights but is ethically demanding
    • Observational continuum
    • Participation: covert vs. overt
  • Ethics are not just procedural but relational and ongoing
  • Online contexts challenge traditional notions of privacy and consent
